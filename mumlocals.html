Mum Locals

I’ve been interning at BARC for over a month, where my daily task involves making composites and testing their properties, measuring exactly how much stress, strain, and impact a material can withstand before it fractures. A world of precise, controlled physics. Then, I leave the lab and step into the Mumbai local, a place that tests human limits in a far less controlled environment. (Explicit dynamics in real world?)

When I first arrived in Mumbai, I was a complete novice to the local trains. I remember standing on a platform that felt like a churning, suffocating vortex. When the train finally chugged in majestically, the pressure from the crowd behind me was absolute. Suddenly, a guy behind me, for reasons only he understands, tickled my ribs. I jerked forward in a frantic, involuntary spasm, stumbled, and watched the train pull away without me. I stood there on the platform, blinking at the empty tracks, completely breathless, and let out a stunned, "WHAAA_----?"

It was funny then, in a terrifying, rookie kind of way. But after a month, the humor has hardened into a raw, grinding reality.

The platform is a high stakes arena where timing is everything. Before the train has even decelerated, the doors transform into a chaotic bottleneck. People don’t wait for safety; they leap out onto the platform while the wheels are still turning, and others surge inward in a desperate, synchronized tide. If you wait for the train to halt to get down, you always hear that sharp, aggressive shout ringing through the air, "Abey Rukne ke baad utrega kya?" ("Will you get off after it stops?"). It is a demand, not a question, because here, time is currency.

Late to work means LOP, Loss of Pay. That is the bottom line. For the people on this train, every second wasted is money stripped from their pockets, and money stripped from the dinner table.

I used to watch the men clinging to the iron door frames and assume it was just reckless stupidity for the momentary thrill of the speeding train. Then, I started looking at the women’s compartment. It is just as flooded, just as dense, and just as intense. But inside, it’s a mesmerizing display of agility. I watch the ladies weave through impossible gaps and scale over benches with the fluid, effortless grace of Spiderwoman. It isn't a display of recklessness. It is a masterclass in survival. They are claiming their space in a city that refuses to give them an inch, navigating a crush that would break anyone else.

Why do they risk it? Why jump into a moving train? Why endure this bone crushing heat day after day?

Most are traveling from the far suburbs, hours away, just to afford a roof over their heads. They endure this because a missing train means a late punch-in, and a late punch-in means they have less to take home to their families. The risk is simply the cost of doing business in a city this big.

Yesterday, a child, no older than ten, wove through the throng of passengers. He carried a heavy bag, selling lemons and cheap notebooks, balancing on the vibrating, uneven metal floor with a poise that had nothing to do with physics and everything to do with necessity. He didn't have the carefree spark of a kid on a swing. He had the weary, focused eyes of an adult. He wasn't playing. He was holding his family’s world together.

In my lab at BARC, I test materials to see when they will finally yield. I look for the crack, the failure, the point of collapse. But on this train, I see a material that defies all my data. I see people who are bent, squeezed, and stressed by the crushing weight of their daily struggle, yet they never break. They carry the load.

I’ve realized that the strongest composites in this city aren't the ones I build in the lab. They are the people riding the 6:00 PM fast train home, holding onto a metal bar for dear life, just so they can get back to the ones who make it all worth it.
